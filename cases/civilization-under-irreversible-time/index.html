No Undo: Civilization Under Bounded Knowledge and Irreversible Time

Who defines interests? How do structural constraints change over time? And can we protect reasonable lives today without exhausting the capacity of future generations to live?

OPEN RESEARCH · HYPOTHESIS NOT ESTABLISHED

Historical evidence: partial. Economic theories: established within their specific scopes. Cross-disciplinary synthesis and causal hypothesis: proposed, not validated. No collapse forecast or political endorsement.

Under bounded knowledge, finite lifetimes, irreversible time and evolving structural constraints, can humanity establish an order in which reasonable lives today and the survival capacities of future generations do not have to remain in persistent conflict?

1. The question behind the dispute

The inquiry began with a dispute over whether powerful actors can rewrite rules. But the deeper question emerged almost immediately: Who gets to define the interests that those rules are supposed to serve? If a powerful actor defines the common good, public rules may become an extension of private advantage. A majority can also impose costs on minorities; technical experts cannot derive ultimate ethical ends from evidence alone. The problem is not solved simply by identifying a more enlightened ruler.

2. Interest is not the same as preference or payoff

A preference describes what an actor wants. A payoff describes an observed or expected consequence of an action. An interest must also account for the actor's future ability to survive, achieve goals and retain feasible options. Value judgments determine which interests merit protection and which trade-offs can be justified. We therefore propose a working definition: An interest is the dynamic effect of feasible actions on an identified actor's present well-being, future survival conditions, goal-achievement capacity and subsequent option set, given historically specific constraints. This definition requires six explicit coordinates: actor, goal, current state, time horizon, binding constraints and externalities. It does not assume that all actors' interests can be converted into one common numerical measure.

3. Social choice cannot supply an infallible definition

Kenneth Arrow's social choice work exposed conditions under which no general aggregation rule can simultaneously satisfy a set of desirable properties for every possible preference profile. Amartya Sen examined welfare, freedom, poverty and social choice beyond simple income aggregates. Mechanism design, recognized by the 2007 economics prize, studies how rules can align incentives and elicit information among agents with different interests. Yet even a well-designed mechanism must specify an objective. Who legitimately sets that objective remains a separate question. These theories explain constraints on collective decisions; none proves that a unique and timeless measure of civilization's welfare exists.

4. The Chongzhen problem: when adjustment may arrive too late

The collapse of the Ming dynasty in 1644 should not be reduced to the personality of its final emperor or a single drought, tax decision or military error. The dynasty inherited institutions and fiscal commitments formed under earlier conditions. Military pressures, administrative constraints and climate-related shocks interacted over time. Historical drought reconstructions help document severe environmental stress in the late Ming, but do not establish that collapse was inevitable or identify one sufficient cause. The analytically important possibility is a time-constrained structural trap: measures that address immediate expenditure requirements can damage the future tax base or cooperation on which recovery depends. Even an informed ruler may face a situation in which available reforms take longer to work than the remaining capacity to finance or coordinate them.

5. Three clocks, not merely a discount rate

Dynamic economic models already analyze time; what a static welfare comparison misses is the possible disappearance of the institutions that would realize future payoffs. We distinguish time to critical-function failure, time for an intervention to become effective and time to restore depleted capacity. Each is uncertain and depends on the actions taken. A negative gap between available time and implementation time signals that the current response path may fail unless it changes the hazard, accelerates implementation or restructures the system. It is not a calibrated countdown to collapse. The relevant empirical work would use event histories, competing scenarios, survival analysis and explicit counterfactuals.

6. Survival of whom?

A ruler may identify survival with a dynasty. An administration may identify it with its organizations. Residents may care primarily about safety, subsistence, family and functioning public services. Future generations inherit environmental, technological, fiscal and institutional conditions. Continuity of a regime is not equivalent to continuity of society or civilization. A system's own persistence cannot be accepted as its highest good without reviving the original problem of who defines interests. Research must distinguish regime survival, institutional continuity, social capabilities and intergenerational opportunities, and identify which populations benefit or bear costs during transitions.

7. A provisional dynamic research framework

Let the social state at time t be x(t), an action or policy rule be a(t), and uncertain shocks be ε(t). A stylized transition is x(t+1) = F(x(t), a(t), ε(t)). Let K denote a specified region in which essential human and cooperative functions remain viable. One possible question is whether a given policy keeps the state within K over horizon H with a clearly stated risk tolerance. But this framework cannot tell us, by itself, who defines K, whose rights constrain policy or what constitutes an acceptable risk. Viability is an analytical constraint, not a moral license to sacrifice people for the preservation of an incumbent authority.

8. Limited knowledge, limited lives, irreversible time

Herbert Simon's bounded rationality reminds us that decision-makers operate with incomplete information and limited cognitive capacity. Historical actors did not know their eventual biographies in advance. Nor do we. Retrospective success does not prove that every earlier choice was justified; failure does not prove that all earlier judgments were irrational. The distinction between decision quality at the time and realized outcome is essential. Waiting for more evidence carries an opportunity cost, while irreversible commitments can destroy future options. Preserving options therefore matters, but cannot be the sole objective: family responsibilities, institutional trust and long-term projects often require binding commitments. The central problem is choosing which commitments are justified despite uncertainty.

9. Generations that cannot negotiate with us

Future people cannot participate in current bargaining, yet they inherit the results of decisions made today. The ethical challenge is symmetrical: current people should not be required to make unlimited sacrifices for speculative future benefits, but should not automatically be entitled to impose irreversible harms on those unable to consent. Intergenerational evaluation requires explicit treatment of substitution, technological change, ecological limits, distribution, representation and enforceable safeguards. A larger present output does not alone establish a larger long-run capability set.

10. Candidate principles, not findings

This inquiry suggests seven principles to investigate: (1) identify the actor whenever an interest is claimed; (2) make the time horizon explicit; (3) disclose structural constraints and costs shifted to others; (4) test whether institutions can recognize and respond to change before critical capacities are lost; (5) distinguish what was knowable ex ante from what became visible ex post; (6) keep the exercise of power contestable and accountable; and (7) allow institutions to be replaced when preserving them ceases to protect the people they purportedly serve. These are proposed research criteria, not independently established universal laws.

11. Open challenges and falsification paths

Can distinct forms of welfare and freedom be compared without collapsing them into a single ruler's preference? Can institutional tipping points be recognized prospectively rather than narrated retrospectively? When does procedural oversight improve adaptation, and when does it obstruct urgent response? Which losses can realistically be substituted or repaired? Who can represent people not yet born? What would comparable historical cases show where regimes survived similar combinations of fiscal stress, environmental shocks and war? The research must include counterexamples and cases of successful adaptation, not only failures such as the late Ming.

Conclusion: no undo, but responsible choice

A civilization need not discover a perfect, timeless arbiter of the common good before people can cooperate. It may instead develop institutions that make interests legible, constrain arbitrary power, expose mistakes, preserve essential human capabilities and change when inherited arrangements cease to work. The distinction is decisive: we are not trying to prove which political order is permanently correct; we are asking whether an order can remain corrigible while protecting reasonable lives today and the conditions for lives tomorrow. This remains an open question. Evidence can narrow uncertainty; it cannot abolish the need to choose.

Evidence boundary and revision policy

HISTORICAL EVIDENCE PARTIAL · THEORETICAL SYNTHESIS PROPOSED · CAUSAL CLAIM NOT ESTABLISHED · OPEN TO REVISION

No individual historical actor's motives are inferred from outcomes. No dynasty's preservation is treated as identical to human or civilizational welfare. Missing evidence is not evidence of nonexistence. This is a research question, not a verified causal model, political monitoring case, or policy prescription.
